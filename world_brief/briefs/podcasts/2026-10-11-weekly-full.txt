PRIYA: It comes down to crude oil and exiting foreign capital. Brent crude remained above one hundred and four dollars a barrel due to Persian Gulf tensions. Since India imports over eighty percent of its crude, high oil prices force Indian companies to buy substantially more US dollars just to pay import bills.
KARTIK: And foreign investors were pulling money out at the exact same time.
PRIYA: In massive amounts. Foreign portfolio investors pulled over forty-four thousand crore rupees out of Indian equities in the first ten days of October alone. When foreign funds sell Indian stocks, they trade those rupees back into dollars to take them home. That twin pressure—dollars needed for oil plus dollars needed by exiting investors—dragged the rupee toward ninety-six rupees and seventy-eight paise.
KARTIK: For anyone living abroad sending money home, a weaker rupee gets you more rupees per dollar. But inside India, it brings imported inflation. How did the RBI step in to counter this?
PRIYA: They launched a three-part defense. First, interest rates. The RBI lifted its benchmark repo rate—the rate at which it lends to commercial banks—to five point five percent. Major state lenders immediately raised their loan rates, which makes borrowing more expensive to cool domestic spending and keep inflation in check.
KARTIK: Second, they set up a dedicated dollar window for state oil refiners. Why bypass the normal market?
PRIYA: When giant refiners like Indian Oil buy billions of dollars on the open market all at once, that concentrated demand causes the rupee to slide sharply. By selling dollars directly to them from foreign exchange reserves, the central bank shields daily currency trading from sudden shocks.
KARTIK: And the third move targeted currency speculators directly.
PRIYA: Right. Starting Monday, the RBI is placing a five-million-dollar daily transaction cap on currency derivative markets. When a currency falls, speculators often use derivatives—contracts betting on future price swings—to wager on further declines. Limiting those daily derivative trades to five million dollars effectively chokes off aggressive speculative short bets against the rupee.
KARTIK: And this energy squeeze extends well beyond India.
PRIYA: Exactly. In the US, high oil is helping hold the ten-year Treasury yield above five point two percent, which keeps mortgage rates and broader borrowing costs elevated.
KARTIK: Turning to the US, there was intense anxiety this week around immigration after a proposal surfaced suggesting a seventy-thousand-dollar fee on OPT work permits for international students. What is the real situation here?
PRIYA: Let's be completely clear: this is strictly a proposal. It has not been approved, it is not an official administrative rule, and it is not law. Nothing has changed for anyone currently on an F-1 visa or working under an OPT or STEM OPT extension today.
KARTIK: So no international graduate needs to worry about paying seventy thousand dollars tomorrow?
PRIYA: Not at all. OPT allows international graduates, especially in STEM fields, to work in the US for up to three years. Restrictionist groups regularly target it. While this seventy-thousand-dollar fee faces immense legal and practical hurdles before it could ever become policy, it does signal that post-graduation work authorization will face heavy political scrutiny in coming legislative talks.
KARTIK: We saw similar headline anxiety around PERM labor certifications this week. What happened there?
PRIYA: Reports spread about the US Department of Labor issuing administrative audit freezes on certain PERM applications, which triggered alarm across Indian tech circles. But major IT companies stepped forward to clarify that current H-1B visas are completely unaffected.
KARTIK: Can you unpack that difference for anyone unfamiliar with the terminology?
PRIYA: A PERM certification is just the first step of an employment-based green card, where the Department of Labor checks whether hiring an immigrant displaces qualified US workers. An audit freeze can slow down your long-term green card timeline, but it does not cancel your current H-1B work visa. Your authorization to live, work, and get paid in the US remains fully valid.
KARTIK: So for an Indian engineer in Dallas, your current status is sound, even if the permanent residency queue slows down.
PRIYA: Exactly. Keep your immediate work status separate from your long-term green card timeline.
